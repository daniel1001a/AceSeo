As of October 30, 2026, BoardFlight (www.boardflight.com) is an active airport-planning website that estimates when a traveler should arrive at a US airport for a particular flight. Its stated purpose is to calculate an airport arrival time based on the flight, terminal, checked baggage, security lane, and expected time needed to reach the gate.

## 1. What is BoardFlight?

BoardFlight is a free flight-arrival planning tool. You enter your flight details, and it works backward from departure to estimate when you should enter the airport.

It models several stages:

- Reaching the check-in area.
- Checking and dropping off baggage, if applicable.
- Clearing the relevant security lane.
- Walking from security to the departure gate.
- Allowing for the airline's boarding cutoff.

It is not an airline, flight-booking service, or live airport-security tracking service. Its output is a planning estimate, not a guarantee.

## 5. Where does BoardFlight get its airport wait time data from?

This is probably the most important question, because it affects how much confidence travelers should place in its estimates.

BoardFlight does not appear to obtain live TSA security-line wait times directly from airports or TSA. It primarily uses its own modeling based on historical and scheduled information.

According to its published methodology, its information comes from several sources.

A. Airport-specific research

BoardFlight says it researches airport information using publicly available airport terminal guides and related operational information.

This includes information relevant to:

- Terminal layouts.
- Security checkpoints.
- Walking distances from security to gates.
- Check-in and baggage-drop procedures.
- Airline boarding and check-in deadlines.

It records airport-specific information rather than simply applying one nationwide estimate.

BoardFlight+1

B. Historical and scheduled security-demand modeling

This is particularly important.

BoardFlight has developed its own models to estimate security waiting times according to the airport and time of day.

Its public data repository describes historical-seed hourly security-demand analysis and a separate schedule-derived curve.

These are modeled estimates, not direct measurements of how long passengers are currently standing in line.

GitHub+1

C. TSA published checkpoint throughput data

BoardFlight uses TSA's published hourly checkpoint throughput figures as an independent validation source.

However, there is an important distinction:

- TSA's data helps BoardFlight check whether its model identifies the correct busy and quiet periods.
- TSA's published throughput figures are not used to establish the actual number of minutes a passenger will wait.
- BoardFlight explicitly says its estimates are not TSA-published wait times.

  GitHub+1

D. Commercial flight-data provider

BoardFlight also uses an unnamed commercial flight-data provider to identify:

- Flight departure airport.
- Departure terminal.
- Scheduled departure time.
- Whether the flight is domestic or international.

The company does not identify this provider by name in its published methodology.

BoardFlight+1

E. Traveler-submitted reports

BoardFlight allows passengers to report their actual security wait times after flying or while waiting in line.

These reports are intended to improve its estimates over time.

However, BoardFlight acknowledges that it currently has too few reports to calibrate its airport estimates.

BoardFlight+1

### The most important limitation: No live security-line data

BoardFlight explicitly states in its methodology:

- It does not have live airport security queue lengths.
- It does not have live camera feeds of security checkpoints.
- Its predictions are based on historical and scheduled patterns.
- Its traveler-reporting system is still developing.

Consequently, a BoardFlight estimate should not be confused with an actual, real-time TSA checkpoint wait time.

However, its security wait times are estimates, not live measurements. Its own methodology also acknowledges limitations in independent validation and traveler-reported calibration. I would use it as an additional planning resource, not as a substitute for airline or airport instructions.
